Phishing: how to spot it and stop it

Phishing is a message designed to trick you into handing over a password, money, or access. It works because it targets people, not software. This 20-minute module shows what attackers send, why it works, and what to do instead.

Quick checklist

Check the sender’s real address, not just the display name. Hover over links before clicking. Be wary of generic greetings, pressure, spelling slips, and unexpected attachments. If a message asks for credentials, payment, or gift cards, stop and verify through another channel.

Recognizing fake websites

A phishing link usually leads to a page that copies a real login screen. The domain is the part directly before the first single “/”, and it’s the one thing the attacker can’t fake.

Anatomy of a deceptive URL

https://login.paypal.com.account-verify.top/signin

Read the domain from right to left. The real domain here is account-verify.top. “paypal.com” is just a subdomain the attacker chose to fool you.

Look-alikes

Swapped characters (rn for m, 1 for l), extra words (-secure, -support), or unusual endings like .top or .xyz.

Padlock myth

HTTPS only means the connection is encrypted. Attackers get HTTPS certificates too, so a padlock doesn’t prove a site is legitimate.

Other red flags

Pages reached from a link instead of your bookmark, login prompts that appear unexpectedly, and forms asking for more than usual (card number, PIN, recovery codes).

Social engineering tactics

Social engineering is manipulation that exploits normal human reactions. Phishing is its most common form. Attackers rarely need to be technical; they need you to act before you think.

Urgency

“Act in 24 hours.” Time pressure shuts down careful checking.

Authority

Messages that seem to come from your CEO, IT, the bank, or the tax office, so you comply without questioning.

Fear

Account locked, legal action, a suspicious login. Panic drives clicks.

Greed and reward

Prizes, refunds, bonuses, or “exclusive” offers that are too good to be true.

Curiosity

“Is this you in this photo?” or “Your parcel couldn’t be delivered.”

Trust and familiarity

Impersonating a colleague or friend, or continuing a real email thread after hijacking an inbox.

Many channels, one trick

Spear phishing

Targeted at one person, using details from LinkedIn, social media, or earlier breaches (your manager’s name, a project you’re working on).

Smishing and vishing

Phishing by SMS (“smishing”) or phone call (“vishing”). Attackers may spoof caller ID and pose as your bank’s fraud team or IT help desk.

Business email compromise (BEC)

An attacker impersonates an executive or supplier and asks for a wire transfer or changed bank details. There is often no link or attachment at all.

Quishing (QR codes)

A QR code in an email, poster, or parking meter that sends you to a fake login page, bypassing link scanners.

MFA fatigue and consent phishing

Flooding you with approval prompts until you tap “Approve,” or tricking you into granting a malicious app access to your account.

Real-world examples

2011

RSA SecurID breach

Employees received emails titled “2011 Recruitment plan” with a spreadsheet attachment that exploited a software flaw. One person opened it, giving attackers a foothold that led to theft of data related to RSA’s authentication products.

2013–2015

The fake supplier invoices

A scammer posed as a hardware manufacturer and sent invoices to Google and Facebook, which together paid more than $100 million before the fraud was discovered. No malware was needed, only convincing paperwork and email addresses.

2016

Podesta email compromise

A spear-phishing message disguised as a Google security alert told Hillary Clinton’s campaign chairman to “change his password.” The link led to a fake login page, and thousands of his emails were later leaked.

2020

Twitter phone-phishing attack

Attackers phoned Twitter employees pretending to be internal IT, then directed them to a fake login portal. They gained access to internal tools and hijacked high-profile accounts to run a cryptocurrency scam.

What these have in common: a believable story, a request to act quickly, and one person who wasn’t sure how to verify. Strong habits (and a company culture where checking is welcome) stop all of these.

If you already clicked

Don’t panic or hide it. Disconnect from the network if you downloaded something, change the affected password (and any reused ones) from a clean device, tell your IT or security team right away, and watch your accounts for unusual activity. Speed matters more than embarrassment.
